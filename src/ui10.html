    How HKOI defeats a laser-guided missile

    A beam-riding missile steers by a receiver in its tail that looks back at its own launcher. Put a cloud on that line and it has nothing to follow.

    1. OvertakeThe laser warner gives the bearing, the radar locks on, and the gun fires a burst timed to pass the missile hundreds of metres out.
    2. BloomProximity and time fuzes burst the rounds just behind the missile, stacking the cloud on its guidance beam.
    3. SeverThe laser can no longer reach the receiver in the missile’s tail. Guidance is gone.
    4. DriftThe missile flies on blind, drifts and sags. Cut far enough out, it misses or hits the ground. Anything left goes to the APS.

    Known limits

    • Nothing here is hardware-tested. This is a simulation at TRL 3. It shows trends and requirements, not proven performance.
    • Close-in launches. A missile cut inside about 400–600 m is still pointed at the vehicle and often hits. Launches from under about 1 km leave too little time; those go to the APS. By default Kornet-class launches start at 2 km; lower “Closest Kornet launch” under All settings to study ambushes.
    • Fire-and-forget IR missiles. There is no beam to cut. HKOI can only try proximity HE. Pick that scenario to see the gap.
    • Unguided rockets. HKOI ignores them by design and leaves them to the APS.
    • Friendly troops close by. If the APS is held to protect dismounts, HKOI is the only layer and survival drops sharply.
    • Detection is the biggest unknown. How far out a laser warner sees a weak guidance beam sets how far out HKOI can meet the missile.
    • Flight physics. From the moment HKOI fires, each guided missile is flown in 3D every 20 ms: a steering autopilot (2.5 rad/s, 0.12 s lag, 15 g limit) holds it on its beam against gravity. The beam is lost when the obscurant column between the missile and its launcher, worked out from the true positions of the drifting, spreading puffs, passes the lock margin. Blind, it keeps its last velocity and flies on under gravity, its own trim and lift, and it can reacquire if the cloud clears while it is within 4 m of the beam. Along-track speed is held constant; the missile's real autopilot gains and beam field are assumptions.
    • Simplifications. One vehicle, statistical terrain masking, a Gaussian puff cloud, a simple crew model (their confidence, stress and reactions are assumptions, not measured behaviour), and no friendly-fire rules. Smoke is assumed not to block the laser warner.

    What to test first

    1. Chamber test of the fill at 1.06 μm. The pass line under “What matters most” gives the number to beat.
    2. Live burst of a 30 mm carrier shell. Measure cloud size and how long it stays dense in wind.
    3. Laser warner against a beam-rider-class source. Measure detection range for a low-power guidance beam.
    4. Fuze function on a crossing target. Confirm the proximity trigger on overtake and the time-fuzed stacking.

    How the model was checked

    • An automated suite fights 7,200 random raids across random setups and checks 25 rules that must always hold: no shot before a radar track, one gun cannot fire two bursts at once, the APS never exceeds its magazine, and so on. All pass.
    • Every replay reruns a counted raid with the same dice rolls and must reproduce it exactly.
    • Comparisons face the same enemy launches, so differences between setups are not luck.

    One mission, step by step

    Every raid runs through this chain. A threat can be stopped at step 6, 7 or 8; if nothing stops it, it hits.

    RaidThe enemy launches missiles or drones from random directions in front of the vehicle, at ranges that depend on terrain and visibility.
    WarningThe laser warner sees a guidance laser, an RF sensor hears a drone's radio, the APS radar spots anything inside about 1 km, and an optional launch warner sees missile plumes. Once the radar is on it also searches for threats nobody warned about.
    Radar trackThe warning points the radar at that bearing. It needs a moment to lock and measure the threat's speed and path.
    PlanThe fire control works out when to fire so the rounds meet each missile at the best distance, and in which order if there are several.
    FireThe gun turns, waits for the right moment, and fires a short burst down the missile's path.
    BloomRounds passing near the missile fuze and burst into an obscurant cloud between the missile and its launcher.
    SeverThe cloud blocks the guidance beam. The missile flies blind, drifts and sags, and usually misses or hits the ground.
    APSAnything still coming is met by the APS right next to the vehicle, which has only a few shots per side.

    Words used here

    Beam rider
    A missile (Kornet, Vikhr) that flies inside a laser beam aimed by its launcher. Block the beam and it is lost. HKOI's main target.
    SAL missile
    Semi-active laser: the missile homes on a laser spot painted on the vehicle. The cloud doesn't help, so HKOI fires HE at it instead.
    SACLOS (radio command)
    The launcher tracks the missile optically and steers it by radio. A cloud can break that optical tracking, but not always.
    FPV / fiber-optic drone
    Small attack drones. HKOI fires proximity HE at them. Fiber-optic ones emit no radio, so only radar can find them.
    LWR
    Laser warning receiver. It tells you a laser is on you and roughly from which direction (about ±8°).
    Crew confidence
    How sure the crew is that an incoming threat will miss, judged a few seconds before impact from what the fire-control display shows, how much they trust HKOI, and how many other threats are in the air.
    Crew stress
    A 0–100 score that rises with warnings, close missiles, APS shots and hits, and falls when HKOI calls a cut or a threat is defeated. Calm below 30, alert to 55, stressed to 80, panic above.
    Evasive drill
    The driver accelerates and turns so the vehicle is no longer where it was. It beats a blind missile, but not one that is still being guided, and it shakes HKOI’s aim.
    Launch warner (MAWS)
    Missile approach/launch warning: an ultraviolet or infrared sensor that sees a rocket motor. Works on every missile, not on drones.
    Radar search
    Once the fire-control radar is emitting it sweeps around the vehicle, so it can find threats that gave no warning, a moment later than a cued lock.
    Follow-up burst
    Another burst at the same threat: after an HE miss, or when a cut missile steers back onto its beam.
    EMCON
    Emission control: the radar stays silent until something is detected, so the enemy can't locate the vehicle by its radar.
    Meet distance
    How far from the vehicle the rounds meet the missile. Too close and a blind missile still hits; too far and the cloud thins before the missile arrives.
    Sever
    The moment the cloud gets thick enough to block the missile's guidance.
    Co-located rounds
    Rounds 2 and 3 are time-fuzed to burst at the same spot as round 1, stacking the cloud thicker.
    Upwind offset
    Aim the extra puffs slightly upwind so the wind carries the cloud across the beam instead of away from it.
    APS
    Active protection system (for example Trophy). It shoots down missiles a few metres from the vehicle; limited shots, needs time to reset.
    Optical depth
    How opaque the cloud is along the beam. 3 means about 5% of the laser gets through, which is assumed to break lock.

    How to read the numbers

    • Most input values are placeholders chosen to explore the design. Trust the comparisons and trends more than any single percentage.
    • The APS-only and no-protection results face exactly the same enemy launches as your HKOI run, so the difference is a fair comparison.
    • With 1,000 raids, differences under about 3 points are random noise. Use 5,000 raids or change the seed to check.
    • A replay reruns one counted mission with the same dice rolls, so what you watch is exactly what was scored.
